# How Therapy-Speak Took Over Everyday Language

## 1. Everyone's an Expert Now

Apparently, everyone's ex is a narcissist these days.

A bad relationship is toxic. A difficult conversation becomes gaslighting. And somewhere along the way, half the internet seems to have developed an avoidant attachment style.

That's an exaggeration, obviously. But the language should sound familiar.

Trauma bonding. Codependency. Emotional availability. Boundaries.

You hear these words in relationship podcasts, celebrity gossip, reality television, and comment sections where strangers seem remarkably confident about people they've never met.

And they don't all mean the same thing. Calling an ex a narcissist isn't the same as diagnosing them with narcissistic personality disorder.

Still, the vocabulary has become so familiar that we barely notice it anymore.

Most of us never formally studied psychology. We didn't spend our weekends reading clinical papers or learning diagnostic criteria.

We picked up the language somewhere along the way.

In 2024, the American Psychological Association published an article about the growing popularity of therapy-speak. It pointed to reality television, where discussions of emotional intelligence, boundaries, and narcissistic tendencies had become part of the entertainment.

*The Bachelor*. *Vanderpump Rules*.

Shows built around ordinary human drama were now borrowing the vocabulary of psychological expertise.

The interesting part wasn't that people were using these terms on television.

It was how little explanation they seemed to need.

## 2. An Old Language, a New Distribution System

In 2022, Merriam-Webster named *gaslighting* its Word of the Year.

Searches for the term on its website had increased by 1,740 percent compared with the previous year.

The word itself was much older.

It traces back to *Gas Light*, a British play from 1938 about a husband who manipulates his wife into questioning her own perceptions. A Hollywood adaptation starring Ingrid Bergman followed in 1944.

Decades later, the expression was being used in conversations about relationships, workplaces, politics, and public figures.

The dictionary figures measured searches, not actual word usage. But they revealed the extraordinary attention an old expression was receiving.

This wasn't the first wave of popular psychological vocabulary.

In a 2025 interview with the American Psychological Association, clinical psychologist Erin Parks recalled how fashionable *codependent* had become in the 1990s.

Back then, self-help books, television therapists, and magazine advice columns were bringing psychological ideas into people's homes.

TikTok didn't invent our fascination with understanding people through psychology.

It gave that fascination a different distribution system.

You can now encounter attachment theory without looking for it.

Maybe you're watching a video about a celebrity breakup. Someone explains why one partner appears avoidantly attached.

Later, the same expression turns up in a Reddit discussion.

Then a friend uses it over dinner.

By that point, it feels like ordinary vocabulary.

Researchers have studied how language spreads through these kinds of social connections.

In 2014, Jacob Eisenstein and his colleagues analyzed roughly 107 million tweets to examine how new expressions moved across the United States.

They found that language changes followed geographic and social patterns. Different communities adopted and spread expressions at different rates.

The research wasn't specifically about therapy-speak, but it showed how online networks can carry vocabulary across communities.

And the words don't necessarily arrive unchanged.

In 2026, computational linguists Alina Iacob and Ana Sabina Uban compared psychology-related terminology in academic literature and Reddit discussions between 2010 and 2025.

They found differences in how many terms were used across professional and public conversations.

Some concepts acquired broader associations as they moved into everyday speech.

That can make language more flexible. It can also make certain distinctions harder to preserve.

Take *gaslighting*.

There's a difference between manipulating someone into doubting their own reality and simply disagreeing with their account of an argument.

Yet online, both situations may be described with the same word.

The popularity of a term, then, tells us relatively little about how precisely people use it.

To understand its appeal, we have to look at what people get from using it.

## 3. Why Finding the Right Word Feels So Good

There's something satisfying about discovering a word for an experience you've struggled to explain.

It gives you a way to communicate what happened. It may help you recognize a pattern or find other people who have dealt with something similar.

That benefit isn't hypothetical.

In 2023, Callie Ginapp and her colleagues published a study based on conversations with 43 adults who reported having been diagnosed with ADHD.

Many described finding something valuable in online communities: people who understood their experiences, practical advice for everyday problems, and words for difficulties they'd previously struggled to explain.

Some also had concerns.

They'd seen misleading information spread online, sometimes by creators with no clinical training. And they worried about how easily complex symptoms could be reduced to short, confident explanations.

Both experiences came from the same study.

For some participants, social media helped them understand their condition. But the same platforms could also expose them to information they didn't necessarily trust.

The right word can make something easier to explain to other people.

And once a word has become part of a community's everyday language, it can also help people recognize shared experiences.

That's one reason psychology terms have staying power.

They're not merely fashionable expressions. For some people, they answer a genuine need.

Yet being useful isn't the only thing that makes specialized language attractive.

Consider these two sentences.

"My coworker is difficult."

"My coworker displays narcissistic traits."

Same coworker. Same evidence.

But the second statement sounds closer to something a professional might say.

A word associated with expert knowledge can change how a speaker is perceived.

In 2020, Zachariah Brown, Eric Anicich, and Adam Galinsky published nine studies examining professional jargon and social status.

They found that, in certain situations, people placed in lower-status positions used more specialized language, partly because they were concerned about how others would judge them.

The research wasn't about therapy-speak. It was about professional communication more broadly.

But it reveals another reason people sometimes adopt specialized vocabulary: the words can signal knowledge and belonging.

If everyone in your social circle talks about attachment styles, understanding that vocabulary helps you participate.

You may use it because it's accurate, because it's convenient, or simply because it's how people around you speak.

These motives can overlap, and they don't require anyone to be consciously trying to impress an audience.

But there's a crucial difference between using a psychological concept to describe your own experience and using it to pass judgment on somebody else.

That difference becomes particularly visible online.

## 4. The Comment Section Doesn't Have Time for Nuance

In 2024, the American Psychological Association reported an observation from psychologist Taisha Caldwell-Harvey.

Sometimes a patient describes their boss as a narcissist.

The patient may feel that the label explains the problem.

Caldwell-Harvey, however, still needs to find out what the boss actually did.

Was it a recurring pattern? What were the circumstances? How did the behavior affect the patient?

Those details matter when trying to understand the situation.

On social media, the conversation can move much faster.

Someone posts about an argument with their partner.

A commenter writes, "Textbook gaslighting."

People immediately recognize the accusation.

The comment doesn't necessarily tell us much about the relationship, but it makes the commenter's judgment unmistakable.

And that matters because online arguments often have spectators.

Sometimes you're not trying to change the other person's mind. You're trying to make your side of the story convincing to everyone watching.

A recognizable psychological term can help do that in remarkably little space.

A person could write three paragraphs describing why a particular action seemed manipulative.

Or they could call it gaslighting.

The shorter version leaves out context, but it delivers a judgment that other people can immediately recognize.

Research suggests that the moral tone of a message can matter to its circulation.

In 2017, psychologist William Brady and his colleagues examined more than half a million tweets about divisive political issues.

They found that posts combining moral judgment with emotional language tended to spread further, especially among people who already shared similar views.

Now, those weren't arguments about narcissism or gaslighting. We can't simply assume that the same effect applies to therapy-speak.

What the study does show is that the language people choose can matter to how an argument travels.

In a 2025 paper called *Unmasking Therapy-Speak*, philosophers Carme Isern-Mas and Manuel Almagro explored how psychological terminology can give subjective judgments the appearance of professional authority.

The paper offers a philosophical argument about what this language can do, rather than an experiment measuring how often it happens.

And that's where psychological labels become interesting.

Calling someone selfish makes your opinion clear. Calling them a narcissist can sound like you've also explained why they behave that way.

Whether you've actually explained anything depends on the evidence.

But for someone scrolling through a comment section, the difference isn't always obvious.

And once people accept the category, they may start interpreting further behavior through it.

A rude comment becomes evidence of narcissism.

An inconsistent memory becomes evidence of gaslighting.

Maybe the original accusation is accurate. Maybe it isn't.

From a short video, a few screenshots, or one person's account, the audience often has very little way of knowing.

Yet a thousand people can agree on the label.

And a thousand people agreeing with a label doesn't necessarily tell us whether it fits.

It tells us that the interpretation has found an audience.

## 5. When Personal Problems Get Personal Solutions

Therapy-speak doesn't only influence how we judge other people. It can also shape the explanations we accept for our own problems.

Consider a worker who regularly puts in sixty hours a week, answers messages on weekends, and struggles to get time off.

She says she's burned out.

Her friends suggest setting boundaries, protecting her energy, and making more time for herself.

Those suggestions may help.

But suppose the employer expects everyone to maintain the same schedule.

What happens when a problem partly created by an organization is addressed mainly through personal behavior?

Burnout is a particularly revealing example.

The World Health Organization classifies it as an occupational phenomenon connected to chronic workplace stress that hasn't been successfully managed.

The WHO doesn't classify burnout itself as a medical condition.

And its definition explicitly connects the experience to work.

Yet popular advice can place much of the responsibility on the employee.

Learn to say no. Take more breaks. Develop a healthier routine.

There may be good reasons to do those things.

But none of them necessarily changes understaffing, unreasonable deadlines, or a workplace culture that expects people to be constantly available.

An individual can make better choices while still facing conditions that remain fundamentally unreasonable.

This doesn't mean psychological explanations are inherently misleading.

Someone can benefit from therapy while also needing better working conditions. A person can struggle with anxiety while facing genuine financial insecurity.

The difficulty arises when one kind of explanation pushes the others out of view.

If we're used to describing a problem through the individual's emotions, the most obvious response may be to help that individual cope.

But sometimes the circumstances themselves need to change.

And those circumstances won't become less important simply because we've found a psychological name for their effects.

## 6. What Changed, and Why It Matters

A psychological term once associated mainly with experts can now appear in a celebrity argument, a workplace complaint, or a conversation between friends.

Its popularity doesn't require everyone to have studied the ideas behind it.

The distribution is easy. The vocabulary is familiar. And people have real reasons to use it.

But the social life of a word changes depending on where it appears.

In a support community, a term may help somebody communicate an experience.

In a public argument, it may help somebody establish a judgment.

In a workplace conversation, it may direct attention toward an individual's feelings rather than the conditions surrounding them.

Those aren't necessarily competing definitions of the word.

They're different things people can accomplish by using it.

And that's what makes the rise of therapy-speak more interesting than a simple story about internet trends or bad psychology.

We've made the vocabulary of psychological expertise widely available. We've also made it part of how ordinary people establish credibility, interpret behavior, and assign responsibility.

A term can be useful in all those settings.

But the authority people hear in it doesn't always come from the evidence of the particular situation.

Sometimes it comes from the word's association with a field of expertise.

Sometimes it comes from how many other people already accept the label.

And sometimes it comes from the relief of finally having a name for an experience.

The word may be the same.

What it allows people to claim can be very different.

That's worth remembering the next time a stranger on the internet seems to have diagnosed someone from a thirty-second clip.
