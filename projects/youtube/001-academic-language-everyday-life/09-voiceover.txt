Ten or fifteen years ago, if someone forgot appointments, missed deadlines, and could never seem to start a task on time, people might have called them lazy, careless, or undisciplined.

If a boyfriend was selfish, manipulative, and impossible to argue with, you might have said he was controlling.

Or toxic.

Or simply an asshole.

If women kept running into the same double standards at home and at work, we might have called those situations sexist.

We still use those words.

But now we have another vocabulary sitting right alongside them.

ADHD.

NPD.

Narcissist.

Gaslighting.

Trauma response.

Attachment style.

Patriarchy.

It’s tempting to say old words are just being swapped out for newer, fancier terms.

But I don’t think that’s quite what’s happening.

ADHD hasn’t “replaced” the word lazy.

Certain behaviors once written off as laziness, carelessness, or poor self-control are now sometimes framed through ADHD.

The real shift isn’t just about words.

It’s about the kind of explanation we reach for.

“Lazy” is mostly a judgment about behavior.

ADHD asks whether there might be a cognitive or clinical mechanism behind some of that behavior.

“Selfish” tells you what someone did.

Calling someone a narcissist seems to tell you something about the kind of person who keeps doing it.

And “patriarchy” takes an experience that might once have looked private and connects it to a larger social structure.

So why are these newer concepts so attractive?

Part of the answer is simple.

They seem to explain more.

Take ADHD.

ADHD is a clinical diagnosis, not another word for being distracted, disorganized, or bad at answering emails.

But online, it has also become a framework for revisiting experiences that might once have been dismissed as laziness, poor discipline, or personal failure.

“I’m lazy.”

“I have no discipline.”

“Why can everyone else just get things done?”

Then someone stumbles across terms like executive dysfunction, time blindness, hyperfocus, ADHD paralysis.

Suddenly, experiences that once felt scattered start to form a pattern.

Imagine someone watching a short video about ADHD paralysis and remembering years of sitting in front of homework, knowing exactly what needed to be done...

and still being unable to start.

For years, maybe that memory meant one thing:

I was lazy.

Now it might mean something else.

Instead of asking:

“What is wrong with me?”

they start wondering whether there is another explanation.

Sociologists call the broader process medicalization: experiences once interpreted mainly through character, morality, or ordinary social expectations begin to be understood through medical or psychological categories.

You can see why the shift is appealing.

“Lazy” sounds like blame.

ADHD at least opens another possibility.

None of this means seeing yourself described in an online post counts as a real diagnosis.

But even the possibility of a diagnosis gives people somewhere to turn.

Something to investigate.

Something to talk through with a clinician.

It may also change how years of frustration are remembered.

I keep seeing the same reaction in ADHD discussions online:

“Wait, this is ADHD too? I thought ADHD just meant being hyperactive or unable to focus.”

That reaction interests me because the label is doing more than naming a symptom.

It is changing the way someone reads their own past.

A recent study examined 600 comments on popular ADHD TikTok videos.

Around 36.7 percent involved people identifying with ADHD-related behaviors.

Another 5.3 percent went further and suggested that they themselves might have ADHD.

That doesn’t tell us TikTok is somehow creating ADHD.

What it shows is how quickly people can take a professional framework and test it against their own life.

Now turn the same process outward.

“My ex was selfish.”

“My ex was a narcissist.”

Those sentences do not land the same way.

The American Psychological Association now discusses this kind of language under the heading of “therapy speak.”

One of its own introductions asks whether your ex might be a narcissist...

or your boss might be gaslighting you.

That vocabulary is no longer niche.

Why does “narcissist” feel so much more powerful than “selfish”?

Because it seems to explain more.

Picture someone after a breakup, searching one strange behavior late at night.

They find “narcissist.”

Then narcissistic abuse.

Then trauma bond.

Then no contact.

And suddenly the whole relationship starts reorganizing itself around one label.

A string of confusing or hurtful incidents begins to look like signs of one underlying personality pattern.

The charm at the beginning.

The contempt later.

The manipulation.

The withdrawal.

The refusal to apologize.

Once there is a category, those events begin to line up.

Real relationships are rarely that clean.

People contradict themselves.

They can be loving and cruel in the same relationship.

Motives overlap.

Memory is unreliable.

Sometimes someone behaves terribly without fitting neatly into a single personality category.

Still, the category is tempting precisely because real life is not.

Once someone becomes “a narcissist,” the past can feel easier to read.

Sometimes the future does too.

Of course he did that.

That is what narcissists do.

It’s worth noting that the way “narcissist” gets used online often has very little to do with a formal diagnosis of NPD.

This impulse — to sort people into types instead of only judging individual acts — is something Foucault wrote about.

He observed a broader shift in modern societies: institutions increasingly judged people not only by what they did, but by whether they were normal or abnormal, healthy or pathological.

That helps explain why “he acted selfishly” and “he’s a narcissist” land so differently.

One describes an action.

The other classifies a person.

I don’t think the interesting takeaway is that diagnoses are fake, or psychiatric categories are simply made up.

The better question is why classification feels so satisfying.

Why does putting a label on someone feel so much like understanding them?

Then there is a third move.

Not from self to other...

but from the individual to the system.

Think about patriarchy.

A woman notices that women in her family do most of the unpaid care work.

At work, women are expected to be agreeable in ways men are not.

Motherhood is treated as a private choice, while work, income, law, and social expectations quietly shape what that choice costs.

Or take something smaller.

A holiday dinner ends.

The men leave the table.

The women stay behind to clean up.

On its own, that might look like a family habit.

Once you have a structural concept, the scene can begin to look like part of something larger.

That’s what structural concepts can do.

They connect experiences that once seemed entirely separate.

A study of “patriarchy” online traced how a dense and contested feminist concept found new life through internet memes and phrases such as “smash the patriarchy.”

A theory that once took pages to unpack could now travel in three words.

So these three examples are doing different things.

ADHD can give someone a new way to understand themselves.

“Narcissist” can give them a way to classify somebody else.

“Patriarchy” can connect a private experience to a wider structure.

Different scales.

Same appeal.

Each one makes something messy feel more legible.

And that is the first answer to why we adopt these concepts.

They offer more than description.

They offer a pattern.

But that raises another question.

Why do some concepts travel so much better than others?

Part of the answer is compression.

A complicated explanation is difficult to repeat.

A label is easy.

“Narcissist” can compress an entire relationship story into one word.

“Anxious attachment” can summarize a pattern that might otherwise take ten minutes to describe.

“Patriarchy” can carry a structural argument in one familiar term.

Compression does not necessarily make an idea false.

But it makes the idea much easier to carry from one person to another — through conversation, headlines, hashtags, memes, search boxes, and short videos.

Then there is recognition.

People rarely adopt a new concept because they have carefully studied the original literature.

More often, they hear somebody describe a life that sounds suspiciously like their own.

“Wait.

I do that too.”

An abstract category becomes personal.

Someone reads one relatable paragraph, and suddenly a messy decade-long relationship has a name.

And then comes search.

Once a term is searchable, learning it is no longer a one-off encounter.

Search “narcissist” and you quickly find narcissistic abuse, trauma bond, no contact, grey rock, covert narcissism.

Search ADHD and you move into diagnostic criteria, personal stories, medication debates, memes, coping strategies, and other people comparing their lives with yours.

The word becomes an entrance.

Not just to information.

To people who already know how to use it.

I notice this especially in relationship discussions online.

A messy argument that might once have been described as “he was unreasonable” can now be interpreted through attachment styles, narcissistic patterns, trauma responses, boundaries, or emotional abuse.

Once you know those frameworks, certain details start jumping out at you.

An awkward dating pattern begins to look avoidant.

An argument begins to look manipulative.

A private frustration begins to look structural.

The concept helps you notice something.

But it also tells you what to notice.

Those two things are not quite the same.

Sometimes a new term gives someone language for a pattern they genuinely could not articulate before.

Sometimes it makes the pattern easier to see than the evidence really justifies.

Probably both happen.

This whole cycle may feel like a product of social media.

But it is far older.

More than sixty years ago, the social psychologist Serge Moscovici studied what happened when psychoanalytic ideas left specialist circles and entered French newspapers, politics, families, and ordinary conversation.

People did not become amateur Freudians.

They picked and chose.

Some ideas stuck.

Others disappeared.

What survived got mixed with things people already believed.

Over time, specialist knowledge became ordinary common sense.

The internet did not create that process.

It accelerated every stage of it.

Expert language can now be reframed almost instantly by therapists, creators, patients, activists, meme accounts, Reddit communities, relationship coaches.

People find a simplified version, see themselves in it, dig deeper, share it, and slowly reshape the term.

The gap between specialist language and everyday speech has collapsed.

And once that happens, concepts can shift very quickly.

Which brings us to the last part of the story.

What happens to a concept after millions of people start using it?

Ian Hacking studied something closely related.

Humans are strange subjects for classification because we can read the labels used to describe us.

A rock does not care what geologists call it.

People do.

Someone may accept a label.

Resist it.

Find other people through it.

Or reinterpret their past because of it.

And those responses can, in turn, change what the label even means.

Hacking called this the looping effect.

You can see an especially vivid version of this when concepts cross languages.

PUA is a good example.

In English, PUA meant “pick-up artist,” part of a dating subculture built around techniques for attracting women.

In Chinese internet culture, the acronym gradually took on a much broader and darker meaning.

It came to refer to emotional manipulation and coercive control.

Eventually, it spread into expressions such as “workplace PUA,” where the dating context has almost disappeared.

The letters survived.

The concept was repurposed.

That is more interesting than simply saying people mistranslated it.

A borrowed term was taken up, stretched, and made useful for describing a different set of experiences.

Gaslighting took another route entirely.

The word did not begin in psychology.

It comes from the 1938 stage play Gas Light, later adapted into films, in which a husband manipulates his wife into doubting her own perception of reality.

Only later did the term move through psychology and into everyday relationship language.

So there is no single pipeline from “academia” into everyday speech.

Some concepts begin in medicine.

Some come from social theory.

Some come from popular culture.

Some cross languages and end up doing a job they never did in the language they came from.

What they share is what happens afterward.

People make them useful.

And in doing so, they change them.

That, to me, is the full cycle.

We adopt these concepts because they seem to explain more than ordinary descriptions.

They spread because they are easy to compress, easy to recognize yourself in, and easy to search.

And once they spread, people reshape them.

The internet did not invent any of that.

It just made the whole cycle faster.

By the time a concept has moved from specialist language to a creator’s video, from the video to a comment section, from there into someone’s self-understanding, and finally into everyday conversation...

it may no longer mean exactly what it meant at the beginning.

Maybe that is the real shift worth paying attention to.

We didn’t just pick up new words.

We adopted new ways to interpret our own experiences.

And once those concepts enter everyday life, ordinary people reshape them right back.

Labels don’t just describe our lives.

They shape what we see.
