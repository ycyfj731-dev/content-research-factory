## Central inquiry

**How do concepts from medicine, psychology, social theory and subculture become everyday language—and what happens to their meaning when ordinary people use them to interpret life?**

## Working thesis

> The internet did not simply popularize academic jargon. It accelerated a broader process in which very different concepts became portable tools for explaining the self, other people and society. That can make experience easier to name—but portability also invites semantic expansion, context loss and overconfident labeling.

## Annotated narration

Ten years ago, if someone couldn't focus, they might have called themselves lazy.

If an ex was selfish and manipulative, they might have said exactly that.

If a woman felt that the rules of work, dating or family life seemed to favor men, she might have described the situation without reaching for a theory.

Today, the vocabulary is different.

ADHD.

Narcissist.

Gaslighting.

Trauma.

Attachment style.

Patriarchy.

And in Chinese internet culture, even **PUA**—originally short for *pick-up artist*—can now describe forms of emotional manipulation and control. [C07]

At some point, ordinary life started sounding strangely conceptual.

But the obvious explanation—that TikTok simply took academic jargon and made it viral—falls apart almost immediately.

ADHD is a clinical diagnosis. [C11]

“Narcissist” sits awkwardly between a personality trait, a clinical disorder and a casual insult. [C12]

Patriarchy has a long history in feminist and social theory. [C06]

Gaslighting began in a play before it became psychological language. [C03]

And PUA did not begin in a university at all. [C07]

So these words did not come from the same place.

What they have in common is what happened **after** they entered everyday speech.

They became tools for interpretation.

And that is the real story.

### 1. The self: ADHD

Start with ADHD.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is a neurodevelopmental disorder. It is not a synonym for being distracted, bored or bad at answering emails. [C11]

But online, ADHD has also become a language of self-recognition.

People watch videos about time blindness, procrastination, hyperfocus or losing objects, and sometimes see themselves in those descriptions.

That can matter.

A person who has spent years thinking, “I'm lazy,” may encounter a different possibility: maybe there is a pattern here worth understanding.

But recognition and diagnosis are not the same thing.

One recent study examined comments on popular ADHD TikTok videos. In a sample of 600 comments, 42 percent were coded as some form of ADHD-related self-attribution or self-diagnosis. [C04]

Another study comparing ADHD content on TikTok and Bilibili found that videos with higher self-diagnosis risk were also associated with higher engagement. But that study was cross-sectional. It cannot tell us that the algorithm caused people to diagnose themselves. [C05]

That distinction matters.

The internet can make a clinical category more visible without making every use of it clinically accurate.

And once a diagnosis becomes part of everyday language, it can do two things at once.

It can help someone ask a better question about themselves.

And it can tempt them to answer that question too quickly.

### 2. The other person: “narcissist”

Now move from explaining the self to explaining someone else.

“My ex is a narcissist.”

That sentence is so culturally familiar that the American Psychological Association used it as one of the opening examples in a 2025 discussion of “therapy speak.” [C13]

The attraction of the word is obvious.

“Narcissist” does more than say someone is selfish.

It suggests a pattern.

A type of person.

A reason the relationship felt the way it did.

And, importantly, a reason that can feel final.

But clinically, narcissistic personality disorder is not simply “a person who treated me badly.” It refers to an enduring pattern of traits and impairment assessed in a psychological evaluation. [C12]

APA experts have warned that casual labeling can collapse that distinction. One person may be arrogant. Another may be emotionally immature. Another may behave badly in one particular relationship. None of those descriptions automatically establish a personality disorder. [C13]

This is where a concept becomes powerful.

A sentence like “he was selfish and dismissive” describes behavior.

“He is a narcissist” describes identity.

It can feel more explanatory because it turns a messy history into a category.

Sometimes that category may point toward something real.

But the category can also stop the inquiry.

Once a label feels like the answer, we may stop asking what exactly happened.

### 3. Society: patriarchy

Then there is a much bigger jump.

From “What is wrong with me?”

To “What is wrong with you?”

To “What kind of system are we living in?”

That is where **patriarchy** enters.

Unlike ADHD or narcissistic personality disorder, patriarchy is not a diagnosis of an individual.

It is a structural concept.

In feminist theory, the term was used extensively to describe systematic forms of women's oppression, although feminists disagreed about exactly how patriarchy worked and later criticized versions of the concept that failed to account adequately for differences of class, race and other forms of power. [C06]

Then something interesting happened.

The term lost some of its academic centrality—but found a new life online.

Rosemary Lucy Hill and Kim Allen describe a resurgence of “patriarchy” in digital culture, particularly through memes such as “smash the patriarchy.” [C06]

That transformation matters because a theory that once required pages of explanation can now travel in three words.

That is enormously useful.

A portable concept allows people to connect events that might otherwise look unrelated: domestic labor, workplace authority, sexual norms, expectations around motherhood, beauty standards.

But portability comes with a cost.

Hill and Allen point out that memes are brief. The shorter the form, the less room there is for theoretical complexity, including intersectional differences and disagreements about what patriarchy actually means. [C06]

The concept becomes easier to carry precisely because some of its baggage has been removed.

That does not make the concept meaningless.

It changes the way the concept works.

It becomes less like a full theory and more like a lens you can hold up to a situation.

### 4. The strange case of PUA

And then there is PUA, which complicates the story even further.

In English, PUA originally referred to the *pick-up artist* scene: a male subculture built around techniques for attracting and seducing women. [C07]

But in Chinese internet culture, the word traveled.

By the late 2010s and early 2020s, Chinese journalism and scholarship were already using PUA in discussions of emotional manipulation, verbal abuse and coercive relationship tactics. [C07]

Today, depending on context, someone in Chinese may say they were “PUA'd” in a relationship or even at work.

That is not merely translation.

It is semantic reconstruction.

The imported term survived, but its everyday meaning expanded far beyond the original subculture.

And PUA gives us a useful warning.

Concepts do not always move neatly from experts to the public.

Sometimes they move from subculture to journalism, from one language to another, from one social problem to a much broader category.

The public does not simply receive language.

It edits it.

### 5. So what is actually happening?

There is a sociological idea that helps here.

Social scientists sometimes call this a **double hermeneutic**: concepts developed to describe social life can be taken up by the people being described, who then use those concepts to understand and change their own behavior. [C09]

Research on the term **emotional labour**, for example, has examined how a social-science concept was appropriated and stretched in everyday use. [C09]

Something similar happens across our examples, even though the origins are different.

A term moves into public life.

People use it because it names something that previously felt vague.

Then repeated use changes the term.

Sometimes the category widens.

Psychologist Nick Haslam and colleagues use the phrase **concept creep** for the historical expansion of some harm-related concepts into broader ranges of experience. [C08]

Again, this does not mean every expanded use is wrong.

Language changes because people need language to do things.

To recognize.

To accuse.

To explain.

To belong.

To tell a story about themselves.

The interesting question is what a word allows you to see—and what it makes easier to ignore.

### 6. Are we becoming more literate, or just better at labeling?

There is a real benefit to this new vocabulary.

Psychological language can reduce stigma and give people ways to talk about experiences they previously struggled to name. APA psychologists have explicitly described that increased accessibility as one of the positive sides of therapy-speak. [C01]

The same can be true outside psychology.

A structural concept like patriarchy can tell someone that an experience they thought was purely personal may have a social dimension.

That can be clarifying.

But every concept is also a compression.

ADHD compresses a complicated clinical pattern into four letters.

“Narcissist” can compress years of relationship conflict into one identity label.

Patriarchy can compress multiple institutions and power relations into a single structural word.

PUA can compress a wide range of manipulative behavior into one imported acronym.

Compression is why these words travel.

And compression is also why they can mislead.

The problem is not that ordinary people are using “big words” they do not deserve to use.

That would be a strange conclusion.

The deeper problem is that a useful concept can begin to feel complete.

A lens can start pretending to be the whole view.

### 7. This did not start with TikTok

And none of this began with short-form video.

Psychological language has been entering popular culture for decades. In the APA's discussion of therapy-speak, experts point to terms like “codependent” becoming part of public vocabulary long before TikTok existed. [C02]

Psychoanalysis gave earlier generations words like ego, repression and projection.

Self-help culture generated its own vocabulary.

The internet did not invent the desire to explain life through concepts.

What digital platforms changed was the speed, scale and visibility of circulation.

And even there, we should be careful.

We have evidence that concepts spread online.

We have evidence that meanings broaden.

We have evidence that some high-risk self-diagnosis content attracts engagement.

What we do **not** yet have is one simple causal story in which “the algorithm” explains everything.

That story would be satisfying.

It would also be weaker than the evidence.

### Ending

So why does everyone seem to speak in concepts now?

Not because ordinary people suddenly became academics.

And not because theory somehow escaped from universities by accident.

These concepts survive because they are useful.

They give shape to experience.

They let us say:

Maybe this is not just laziness.

Maybe this is not just one bad boyfriend.

Maybe this is not just my private problem.

But the moment a concept becomes portable, it also becomes editable.

Its meaning can widen.

Its boundaries can blur.

Its history can disappear.

And perhaps that is the trade.

The more easily an idea can travel, the less of its original context it can carry with it.

So the question is not whether we should stop using words like ADHD, narcissist or patriarchy.

The better question is whether we can use a concept without letting the concept do all of our thinking for us.

Because a good concept should help us see more.

Not give us a reason to stop looking.
