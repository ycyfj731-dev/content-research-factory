**Title:** How Internet Language Changed the Way We Understand Ourselves  
**Thumbnail:** `LABEL OR LANGUAGE?`

Viewer question: are online labels helping us understand experience, or flattening it?

Promise: examine benefits and costs of popularized concepts.

## Provisional selection

**Selected: Pair B.**

Working title: **Why Does Everyone Speak in Concepts Now?**

Reason: it captures the genuinely broader episode, rather than collapsing it into therapy-speak. It also lets PUA function as a cross-language counterexample and patriarchy as a social-theory case.

## Hook alternatives

### Hook 1 — language changed
Ten years ago, someone who couldn't focus might call themselves lazy. Someone trapped in a confusing relationship might say their partner was manipulative. Today, the vocabulary is different: ADHD. Gaslighting. Trauma. Attachment style. Patriarchy. In China, even PUA has become a verb for forms of emotional control. These words did not come from the same place. So why do they now live in the same place—ordinary conversation?

### Hook 2 — false assumption corrected
It is tempting to say TikTok turned academic jargon into everyday language. But that story breaks almost immediately. ADHD is a diagnosis. “Narcissist” sits between personality language, clinical diagnosis and everyday judgment. Patriarchy is a social theory with a much longer political history. And PUA came from pickup-artist subculture. What the internet did was not invent these concepts. It gave very different concepts a new life as tools for interpreting everyday experience.

## Promise check

The selected title does **not** claim:
- that every concept is academic;
- that social media alone caused the change;
- that all meanings are being misused;
- that widespread use is necessarily harmful.

Those stronger claims are not supported and are excluded from packaging.
