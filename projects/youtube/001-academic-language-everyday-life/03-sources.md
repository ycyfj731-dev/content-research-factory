## Evidence map by case

### ADHD
Supported:
- ADHD is a clinical/neurodevelopmental diagnosis, not merely a synonym for distraction.
- Popular TikTok content is associated with substantial self-attribution/self-diagnosis language in sampled comments (S05).
- A 2026 TikTok/Bilibili study found a correlation between self-diagnosis-risk content and engagement (S06).

Not supported strongly enough:
- “TikTok caused the rise in ADHD diagnosis.”
- “The algorithm intentionally promotes misinformation.”
- “Most young adults self-diagnose ADHD.”

### Narcissist / narcissism
Supported:
- APA's 2025 therapy-speak episode treats “my ex is a narcissist” as a representative example of contemporary popular psychological labeling (S18);
- NPD is a clinical personality disorder with enduring patterns and formal evaluation requirements; casual selfishness is not equivalent to diagnosis (S19);
- APA experts explicitly warn that casual “narcissist” labeling can flatten relational nuance and is often used without knowledge of the clinical definition (S18).

Boundary:
- distinguish **narcissistic traits**, **narcissism as a personality dimension**, and **narcissistic personality disorder**. The episode should not imply that every popular use of “narcissist” claims a formal diagnosis.

### Gaslighting — supporting example, not a main case
Supported:
- term derives from the 1938 play and later film adaptations (S01);
- APA notes the term is now used more generally than its earlier extreme sense (S01);
- useful in the opening montage and as proof that not all “therapy-speak” came from clinics.

### Patriarchy
Supported:
- Hill & Allen explicitly document an online resurgence and changing everyday meanings of “patriarchy,” including meme circulation (S07);
- their abstract describes earlier feminist theorization and subsequent decline in academic fashion before digital resurgence.

Boundary:
- current access is abstract-level. Do not make detailed claims about their meme corpus or specific causal mechanism beyond what the abstract states.

### PUA
Supported:
- PUA originally refers to pickup-artist culture/subculture (S11, S14);
- Chinese sources document strong association with emotional manipulation/control (S11–S15);
- Chinese scholarship by 2019–2020 already analyzed PUA as a gendered manipulation/control practice (S12–S13).

Boundary:
- “PUA means gaslighting in Chinese” is too absolute. Better: **on Chinese social media it has broadened far beyond the original pickup-artist sense and is often used for emotional manipulation/control.**

## Cross-case mechanisms

### M1 — Naming / recognition
Professional or theoretical vocabulary can provide names for experiences that users already recognize. Evidence is strongest as qualitative/expert interpretation (S02, S09), not a universal causal law.

### M2 — Lay appropriation / double hermeneutic
S09 provides a strong sociological precedent: concepts created by social scientists can be appropriated by lay actors and then shape lay self-understanding.

### M3 — Semantic expansion / concept creep
S08, S10 and related corpus research support the broader phenomenon that harm/mental-health concepts can expand in scope. This is useful for trauma/mental-health language, but should not be forced onto every case.

### M4 — Platform portability
Short, memorable labels are evidently suited to posts/memes, and S07 directly discusses meme brevity in the patriarchy case. However, a general causal claim that “algorithms prefer theory words” is not established by the current evidence.

## Rejected / deferred claims

- “All four terms came from academia.” — **REJECTED.**
- “They all became popular at the same time.” — **REJECTED; no comparable time-series evidence.**
- “TikTok algorithms caused their popularity.” — **REJECTED as causal wording.**
- “People today are more psychologically literate than ten years ago.” — **DEFERRED; requires population-level evidence.**
- “The internet made complex theories simpler because people became less intelligent.” — **REJECTED.**

## Research conclusion

The strongest defensible frame is **concept migration**, not “academic jargon suddenly exploded.”

Different concepts travel through different routes, but once adopted by lay users they can:
1. name experiences,
2. become portable interpretive tools,
3. be recontextualized,
4. broaden or shift in meaning.

This supports an essay about knowledge moving between expert and everyday worlds without pretending there is one universal pipeline.
