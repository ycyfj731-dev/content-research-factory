# Philosophy 300 — Source Discipline v0.2

Status: **FROZEN SOURCE GATE**

## Core rule

> 对古代文本、后世转述和现代研究，先忠实呈现来源实际说了什么，再解释。不要为了“好读”自行压缩成一句漂亮总结。

## Source hierarchy

### A. Primary / surviving ancient evidence
Use when available:
- surviving fragments;
- direct works;
- dialogue passages;
- poems;
- speeches;
- inscriptions where relevant.

### B. Ancient testimony
Use when the original work is lost or incomplete.

Always identify:
- who is reporting;
- how much later the report is;
- whether the wording is direct quotation, paraphrase, or classification.

### C. Modern scholarship
Use to:
- reconstruct incomplete evidence;
- explain disputes;
- identify retrospective categories;
- check historiographical assumptions.

Do not turn a scholar's nuanced discussion into a stronger slogan.

### D. Course bridge
A pedagogical question used by the course.

Never attribute a course bridge to the historical thinker.

---

## Quote-first rule — HARD

When a source materially supports a key historical or interpretive claim:

1. locate the exact passage;
2. preserve the source's actual wording and scope;
3. if useful, quote a short passage directly;
4. then explain it in plain Chinese;
5. clearly mark the explanation as explanation.

Do **not** replace the source with a model-written slogan.

Forbidden pattern:
> Source says A+B+C → writer invents a polished sentence D → D is cited as if the source said it.

Example of failure:
> “神话退场，理性登场会过于整齐。”

If no source uses that formulation, do not write it as sourced scholarship.

---

## No compression invention — HARD

Do not compress several source claims into a new metaphor, slogan, binary, or evaluative phrase unless:
- the user explicitly asks for synthesis; and
- the synthesis is labeled as the course's own summary.

Even then, prefer ordinary literal Chinese.

Do not invent phrases such as:
- “历史被讲得太整齐了”
- “神话退场，理性登场”
- “他成了一个开头”
- “塑造了哲学的第一坐标”

unless the wording is itself sourced or clearly marked as editorial paraphrase and genuinely needed.

---

## Required distinctions

Keep separate:
- direct quote vs paraphrase;
- original fragment vs later testimony;
- thinker vs later commentator;
- ancient report vs modern reconstruction;
- historical person vs literary character;
- current scholarly consensus vs contested reading.

---

## Current-source rule

For living reference resources, prefer the current official page.

Do not cite an old archive merely because it appeared in an earlier search.

If date matters, distinguish:
- page/edition date;
- last substantive revision.

---

## Famous-quote rule

A famous phrase is not automatically authentic.

Before presenting it as a quote:
1. locate the earliest surviving source;
2. verify wording;
3. determine whether it is direct, paraphrased, or later formulation.

If uncertain, use indirect speech.

---

## Motivation rule

Evidence that X held a view does not automatically tell us why X held it.

Write motive only when:
- the thinker states it;
- an ancient source credibly reports it;
- or modern reconstruction is explicitly labeled as reconstruction.

---

## Retrospective-category rule

Later philosophical vocabulary may help explain an earlier thinker, but must not be presented as the earlier thinker's own explicit conceptual system without evidence.

Example:
Aristotle's language of first principles may structure his report of Thales. That does not prove Thales himself formulated an Aristotelian theory of “principles”.

---

## Relevance-to-question rule — HARD

Every source paragraph must help answer the lesson's central question.

Do not let a secondary source issue become the article's new subject.

For Lecture 002:
- central question: why later tradition places Thales first;
- source-transmission problems are supporting evidence, not a replacement topic;
- “myth vs reason” belongs to Lecture 003;
- “why water” belongs to Lecture 005.

If a source paragraph is accurate but does not advance the current lesson question, move or delete it.

---

## Final source check

Before publication ask:
1. Did the source actually say this?
2. Is the quoted wording exact?
3. Is my Chinese explanation weaker/equal in strength to the source, not stronger?
4. Did I silently combine multiple claims into a new slogan?
5. Is this source paragraph necessary for the lesson question?

Any failure = revise.
