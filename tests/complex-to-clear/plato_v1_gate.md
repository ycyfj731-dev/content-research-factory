## Source anchors

1. **Crito 47a–48a** — Socrates argues that on questions such as justice one should not follow the opinion of the many simply because they are many; the comparison is with attending to the judgment of the knowledgeable person. The tested hook over-generalized this into a universal theory of truth.
2. **Republic II 357a–367e, especially 359c–360d** — Glaucon's Ring of Gyges challenge: whether justice is worth choosing when injustice can be committed without penalty or loss of reputation.
3. **Republic VII 514a ff.** — the prisoners are in the cave **from childhood**, not explicitly from birth. Strict source fidelity catches this.
4. **Phaedo 74a–76e** — sensible equal things prompt the argument concerning Equality itself and recollection. The lesson must preserve that the passage is part of the recollection / pre-existence argument, not merely a modern concept-recognition puzzle.
5. **Symposium 199c–201c; 201d ff.** — Socrates argues that desire is for what one lacks, while present possession can generate desire for its continuation. The tested hook wrongly made “love means possession” sound like the source premise.
6. **Republic IV 439e–441c** — Leontius' conflict over looking at corpses is used in the argument distinguishing appetite, spirit, and reason. Do not reduce it to a generic “reason vs desire” story.
7. **Republic VII 519c–521b** — the best rulers are compelled to return to political rule; Socrates explicitly contrasts reluctant rulers with those who fight for office. Treat this as Plato's normative argument, not an empirical law about all politicians.
8. **Republic VIII 557a–564a** — Plato connects unrestricted freedom and the democratic character's undisciplined appetites with a transition toward tyranny. Must distinguish ancient Athenian democracy and Platonic psychic freedom from modern liberal-democratic concepts.
9. **Republic IV 433a–444e** — justice is ultimately located in the order of the soul, not exhausted by outward non-criminal conduct. The lesson must present the stronger conclusion as a Platonic inference, not as a quoted sentence.
10. **Phaedo 61c–69e** — philosophy is presented as a practice of dying / death in the context of separating soul from bodily distractions and purification. “Learning how to face death” psychologizes and narrows the argument.

## Required rewrites

### 01
Current:
> 如果一万个人都说你错，一个人说你对，人数能决定谁对吗？

Rewrite:
> **在“什么是正义”这种问题上，为什么苏格拉底宁愿听一个真正懂的人，也不听一万个人？**

Reason: keeps the claim inside the ethical / expertise context of the Crito instead of turning it into the universal slogan “the majority cannot determine truth.”

### 03
Current:
> 如果你从出生起只见过影子，你会不会把影子当成现实？

Rewrite:
> **如果你从小只见过影子，你会不会把影子当成现实？**

Reason: Republic VII says the prisoners have been there from childhood. The conceptual hook remains intact.

### 05
Current:
> 如果爱意味着“拥有”，为什么得到以后，人还想继续拥有？

Rewrite:
> **如果欲望总是指向自己还没有的东西，那我们为什么还会渴望已经拥有的东西？**

Reason: this reproduces Socrates' actual problem more closely. His answer is that the possessor desires continued possession in the future.

### 10
Current:
> 为什么苏格拉底说哲学是在学习如何面对死亡？

Rewrite:
> **为什么苏格拉底说，真正的哲学家一生都在“练习死亡”？**

Reason: preserves the strangeness of the Phaedo's claim instead of immediately converting it into modern emotional preparation for mortality. The lesson must then explain what “practice of dying” means in the dialogue and explicitly distinguish it from suicide.
